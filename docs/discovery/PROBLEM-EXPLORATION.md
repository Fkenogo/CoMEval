# CoMEval problem exploration

**Status:** Working draft for Founder-led discussion  
**Updated:** 22 November 2026  
**Authority:** Founder observations in the CoMEval product-definition conversation  
**Purpose:** Clarify the problem before defining the product, constitution or engineering scope

## How we are developing CoMEval

CoMEval is being explored within Miledge Ventures as a possible shared platform for monitoring and evaluation (M&E). The CoMEval repository is where its product problem, decisions, and eventual product foundation are developed. Miledge Ventures remains the home for portfolio-level strategy and for deciding how this product fits the wider venture portfolio.

We will develop the product through Founder-led discussion. We will bring forward lived experience, compare it with the two project use cases and the existing prototype, separate observations from assumptions, and record agreed definitions and decisions here as they emerge. The prototype gives us an experience to examine. It does not settle the problem or product scope by itself.

The current working order is:

1. Define and validate the problem.
2. Identify the people and organisations experiencing it, their current process, and consequences.
3. Agree core terms and boundaries.
4. Establish a CoMEval constitution with its durable principles and authority.
5. Develop the product foundation and use-case journeys.
6. Reconcile the prototype as an experience reference and identify gaps.
7. Authorise engineering only when the Founder has approved the relevant foundation and scope.

The two project owners are expected use-case partners. Each owner should access their own project. Their involvement in validation and any use of their project materials or data will be agreed with them as the work develops.

## Founder-described experience

These are problem observations from the Founder’s direct involvement in M&E work, including the two projects being considered. They are the starting evidence for discussion. The frequency, relative impact, and variation between settings remain to be explored with the project owners.

### 1. Evaluation becomes retrospective

Evaluation often happens well after implementation, and may be treated as an activity near the end. By then teams face a larger task to reconstruct what was done, when and where it happened, who was reached, and what evidence supports the account. Beneficiary tracing and follow-up become harder. Missing or scattered records limit what an evaluation can confidently establish about outcomes and impact.

### 2. Monitoring and evaluation are disconnected in time

Implementation activities, reports, and feedback are produced while a project is active. If these are captured and reviewed progressively against the project’s measures, teams could monitor delivery and build evidence for evaluation during implementation. A later evaluation could focus on unresolved questions and evidence gaps instead of recreating the whole history.

### 3. Related M&E work is isolated

M&E for one project is often treated as a complete standalone exercise. Reports do not reliably connect to one another. Trends may need to be transferred manually to the next project. A subsequent project may use similar interventions, variables, or measures, yet starts again with separate tools and analysis.

### 4. Baselines and later measures are disconnected

A baseline is often treated as a separate reference point rather than the start of a continuing record. Teams may need to manually compare later data to the baseline or previous rounds. This makes change over time harder to track and increases repeated work.

## Problem statement to refine

> Programme teams struggle to maintain a reliable, evidence-backed account of implementation and change over time. Because field activity, feedback, reports, baselines, and evaluations are handled as disconnected inputs or isolated exercises, teams later spend too much effort reconstructing what happened and linking reported results to their sources. This weakens ongoing monitoring, follow-up, comparison over time, and the eventual evaluation.

This wording is a discussion draft. In particular, “reliable,” “too much effort,” “weakens,” and the relative importance of these consequences need concrete examples from the two project contexts.

## Product opportunity hypothesis

A digital platform could help a project build its M&E picture progressively. It could take in project documents and recurring implementation material, connect relevant reports and feedback to the project’s measures, preserve where each piece of information came from, and support review and approval before it is used in an output. This could make evaluation an ongoing process during implementation, with a later phase focused on remaining information gaps.

Across related projects, the platform could retain comparable measures and prior results so that a new project can learn from earlier work, reuse or adapt instruments, and follow trends. Reuse must preserve the source project, time period, definition, and context so that changed or incomparable measures are not silently treated as equivalent.

These are opportunity hypotheses, not approved features or technical requirements. “Consumes project documents,” “tracks all variables,” “verifies sources,” and “automatically carries trends forward” each need discussion about user control, evidence quality, comparability, privacy, and what the system can actually establish.

## Important distinction to examine

The platform can preserve a source, show its relationship to a measure, and record who reviewed or approved the information. That is different from independently proving that a source or statement is true. We need to define what “verified and approved” means for different source types, who has authority to do it, and how disagreements or later corrections are represented.

Likewise, connecting projects must not imply that all measures can be combined. A measure may change meaning, population, instrument, method, or context between projects. Trend reporting requires explicit comparability rules and should retain each project’s definitions and source lineage.

## Use cases informing the discussion

- **ChildFund livelihoods evaluation:** a baseline-to-endline evaluation with household and youth measures, project reporting, and supporting evaluation material.
- **Omugo multisector project:** multiple sectors and respondent groups, survey tools, periodic reporting, logframes, and endline material.

These are distinct contexts through which to examine the problem. They do not yet establish that the two owners share the same workflow, priorities, or solution requirements. Keep their project information and access scoped to the respective owner.

## Questions for the next discussion

Begin with the retrospective reconstruction problem:

1. In one concrete example, what record or evidence did the evaluation team need but have difficulty finding?
2. Who needed that information, and what action or conclusion was delayed or weakened?
3. Was the information never collected, collected but scattered, collected in a format that could not be connected to the measure, or collected without a clear source or approval trail?

Use a specific example from either use case. Then test whether the same problem appears in the other context before expanding the definition.
